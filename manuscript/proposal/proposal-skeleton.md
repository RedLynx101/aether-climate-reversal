# AETHER Proposal Skeleton

## Working Title

AETHER: Atmospheric Engineering Through High-Energy Removal

## Abstract Draft

AETHER will evaluate whether climate reversal can be treated as a measurable industrial scaling problem. The research will model the conditions under which durable carbon dioxide removal could exceed ongoing emissions and lower atmospheric CO2 over time. The project will link carbon stock-flow accounting, clean-energy requirements, storage durability, robotics and automation cost curves, and measurement/governance constraints. The goal is not to argue that carbon removal is currently sufficient. The goal is to identify what would have to be true for atmospheric CO2 to become a managed infrastructure variable rather than an uncontrolled accumulation problem.

## Research Question

Under what technical, economic, and governance conditions could AI-enabled automation and abundant clean energy make durable net-negative CO2 feasible at civilization scale?

## Motivation

Climate discourse often swings between austerity and denial. Both frames can miss the engineering question. CO2 is a physical stock-and-flow problem, but solving it requires more than a slogan about technology. It requires energy, capital, machines, storage, monitoring, liability, and governance that can keep fake progress from replacing real tonnes.

This project takes the abundance thesis seriously enough to test it. If the thesis fails under realistic assumptions, that matters. If it works only under narrow conditions, those conditions should be visible. If robotics and AI materially change the cost curve, the models should show where and how.

## Literature Areas

- Atmospheric CO2 and carbon-cycle response.
- Carbon dioxide removal pathways and durability.
- Energy-system growth and clean-electricity costs.
- Industrial learning curves and infrastructure deployment.
- Robotics, embodied AI, and automation economics.
- Measurement, reporting, verification, and carbon-credit governance.
- Commons ownership, public trust doctrine, carbon dividends, and planetary-sink governance.

## Method

1. Build a source-backed parameter database.
2. Construct a carbon stock-flow model.
3. Model major durable CDR pathways by cost, energy, scale, and storage constraints.
4. Model robotics and automation as a cost/productivity modifier, not as magic.
5. Generate conservative, base, aggressive, and failure-case scenarios.
6. Explore a governance branch where atmosphere, oceans, and lakes are treated as citizen-owned or trust-held commons, with payments for permitted outputs and restrictions for dangerous outputs.
7. Produce figures that show atmospheric CO2 trajectories, required clean energy, removal costs, and bottleneck sensitivity.

## Expected Contribution

The contribution is a transparent feasibility map. The project should make clear which assumptions drive the result, which carbon-removal pathways matter, how robotics could change the buildout timeline, and where governance failures could invalidate apparent progress.

The governance contribution may include an exploratory commons-ownership path. The point is not to make citizen ownership the core answer by default. The point is to test whether the "nobody owns the atmosphere or ocean" problem can be made more concrete through public trusts, citizen dividends, pollution rents, liability, and strict prohibitions on outputs too dangerous to price.

## Risks and Limitations

- Future AI and robotics trajectories are uncertain.
- Carbon-cycle modeling can be oversimplified.
- Social-media and company claims may overstate real deployment.
- Removal cost curves may not follow software-like dynamics.
- Storage and MRV may become harder at scale.
- Commons ownership may become a license to pollute if prices are too low, measurement is weak, or dangerous outputs are not restricted.
- Climate reversal does not reverse all climate damage immediately.
- CO2 is not the only climate variable.

## Deliverables

- Research proposal.
- Source register and annotated bibliography.
- Model specs and later executable notebooks.
- Scenario figures.
- Draft academic manuscript.
