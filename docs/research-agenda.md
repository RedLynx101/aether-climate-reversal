# AETHER Research Agenda

Project title: AETHER: Atmospheric Engineering Through High-Energy Removal

## Purpose

AETHER asks whether climate reversal can be modeled as an industrial scaling problem rather than only as a mitigation problem. The question is not whether carbon removal is cheap or sufficient today. It is whether credible trajectories in AI, robotics, clean energy, measurement, and storage could make durable net-negative CO2 possible at civilization scale.

## Central Research Question

Under what conditions could durable carbon removal capacity exceed ongoing CO2 emissions enough to lower atmospheric CO2 over time?

## Subquestions

1. What removal rates matter?
   - Estimate drawdown requirements in GtCO2/year under different emissions futures.
   - Translate removal rates into atmospheric ppm changes with carbon-cycle rebound caveats.

2. What energy is required?
   - Compare thermodynamic lower bounds with practical energy intensity.
   - Separate capture energy, compression, transport, conversion, storage, monitoring, and replacement energy.

3. What forms of storage are plausible?
   - Compare geologic storage, mineralization, biochar, bio-oil, biomass, ocean alkalinity, solid carbon, and long-lived materials.
   - Track density, durability, monitoring burden, leakage risk, cost, land use, water use, ecological risk, and liability.

4. Can AI and robotics change the physical cost curve?
   - Estimate robot unit cost, duty cycle, maintenance, payload, task speed, learning rate, and useful life.
   - Compare humanoids with specialized industrial automation. Humanoids may matter for flexible labor substitution, but mining, drilling, construction, sensing, logistics, and factory automation may dominate the carbon-removal buildout.

5. Which assumptions dominate?
   - Clean energy cost.
   - Learning rates.
   - Storage availability.
   - MRV integrity.
   - Capital cost.
   - Supply-chain constraints.
   - Permitting and governance.

6. Can a public carbon utility change the incentive structure?
   - Explore whether atmosphere, oceans, and lakes can be treated as citizen-owned or trust-held commons.
   - Model emission and discharge rights as paid use of shared planetary sinks, with restrictions for dangerous outputs.
   - Test whether a public operator can set a net atmospheric budget, meter use, charge for permitted loading, procure durable removal, and return value to citizens without authorizing more loading than the physical system can manage.

## Thesis Boundary

The project should avoid two weak positions:

- Doom by assumption: treating climate as unsolvable before modeling industrial and technological change.
- Optimism by assumption: treating future AI and robotics as a magic wand without cost, energy, materials, governance, or verification constraints.

The useful contribution sits between them: define the feasibility boundary clearly enough that a reader can see what would need to be true.

## Academic Contribution

A strong version of this project could contribute:

- A transparent scenario model linking removal scale, energy availability, robotics productivity, storage constraints, and atmospheric outcomes.
- A structured comparison of storage states and conversion pathways.
- A cost-curve treatment of automation and robotics as a physical deployment accelerator.
- A sober governance/MRV failure-mode analysis.
- A governance design for a citizen-held or trust-administered public carbon utility, including how industrial users would pay for permitted net atmospheric use and how revenue would fund system capacity and public benefit.
- Figures that show when the abundance thesis works, when it fails, and which parameters control the result.
