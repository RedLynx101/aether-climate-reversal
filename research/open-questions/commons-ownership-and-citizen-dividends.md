# Public Carbon Utility and Citizen Ownership

## AETHER Governance Hypothesis

AETHER proposes that atmospheric carbon capacity be administered as a public service held for citizens through a trust, statutory utility, or comparable commons institution. This is not a claim about current law. It is the project's working institutional hypothesis and a research agenda for testing whether a physical removal system can be paired with an intelligible incentive structure.

CO2 is not treated here as a moral category. Industrial processes can be useful, and some emissions burdens may be worth carrying. The problem is that atmospheric capacity is finite and usually unmetered at the point of use. A public operator would define an atmospheric operating band and net carbon budget, meter additions and verified durable removals, charge for permitted net use, and procure the infrastructure required to keep the balance.

Carbon charges would reflect marginal removal, storage, measurement, liability, scarcity, and systemic risk. Revenue could fund removal capacity, long-duration monitoring, liability reserves, affected communities, and citizen dividends. Local co-pollutants and high-risk releases would remain subject to separate restrictions; a carbon payment would not make them acceptable.

## Why This Might Matter For AETHER

AETHER is partly a physical modeling project, but the physical system will not scale coherently without an incentive structure. If citizens hold the atmospheric service through a trust, using it becomes a priced, measured transaction rather than an unbounded externality. That structure can allow useful industrial activity while making the cost of maintaining the shared system visible.

This could connect to:

- Carbon fees or dividends.
- Cap-and-dividend systems.
- Atmospheric trust litigation.
- Public trust doctrine.
- Ocean commons governance.
- Natural-capital accounting.
- Pollution permits with citizen revenue.
- Liability for ecosystem and climate damages.
- Restrictions on outputs that cannot be made safe by payment alone.

## Questions To Explore

- What would it mean legally to say citizens own the atmosphere, oceans, lakes, or climate-stability interests?
- Would ownership be direct, held by a public trust, represented by sovereign governments, or represented by a new institution?
- Who gets paid: current citizens, affected local communities, future generations through a trust, or some combination?
- How should rates be set for CO2, methane, heat, acidification, nitrogen runoff, toxins, plastics, aerosols, and other outputs?
- Which outputs should be priced, and which should simply be restricted because the danger is too high?
- How would payments interact with carbon removal credits?
- Could a citizen-owned commons model reduce fake credits by making liability and measurement more concrete?
- Would this create perverse incentives, such as governments becoming dependent on pollution revenue?
- How does the budget prevent wealthy actors from buying more net use than the removal system can manage?
- Can this work internationally, given that oceans and atmosphere cross borders?

## Relation To Existing Concepts

This track should review:

- Public trust doctrine.
- Common-pool resource governance.
- Cap-and-dividend.
- Pigouvian taxes.
- Carbon fee and dividend.
- Sovereign wealth funds from resource rents.
- Alaska Permanent Fund-style dividend logic.
- Atmospheric trust proposals.
- Ocean commons and law-of-the-sea governance.
- Rights-of-nature and ecosystem personhood proposals.

Those ideas are not automatically correct. The point is to see whether any of them can be turned into a serious mechanism for pricing use of shared planetary sinks while keeping high-risk outputs restricted.

## Failure Modes

- A weak price or oversized budget can authorize more net loading than the physical system can reverse.
- Poor measurement could make the system just another fake-accounting layer.
- Rich emitters may pay while vulnerable communities still bear local damage.
- Global commons ownership may be hard to enforce across borders.
- Citizen dividends could become politically popular while net atmospheric loading remains too high.
- Governments or trusts could become dependent on pollution revenue.
- Future generations are difficult to represent fairly.

## Working Position

This is now part of AETHER's governing design hypothesis, but it is not established policy, law, or economics. The strongest version does not say "pay citizens and everything is fine." It asks what institutional design keeps permitted net atmospheric use inside verified system capacity, pays for maintenance and removal, preserves useful industrial production, protects communities from local harms, and gives the public real authority over the balance.
